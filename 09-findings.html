The findings

1. Reversibility costs 1.22× per step and is free in loss terms

0.022% from baseline after 50M tokens, for 51.7% less memory at batch 8. The gradient gate predicted it at 4.090e−06 relative difference on one batch; the full run confirmed it over 6,104 steps.

2. Midpoint is reversible, euler is not, and the reason is structural

Euler's inverse is an implicit equation with no closed form — f is evaluated at the unknown. Its error is identical in fp32 and bf16 to three significant figures across a 32,768× change in machine epsilon. Method error, not precision error.

3. Euler is the best forward rule and the worst reversible one

It beat baseline by 1.10% on final loss while being 65% wrong on reconstruction. Reconstruction quality and optimisation quality are independent properties.

4. The reversible stack requires fp32

bf16 degrades the reconstruction by 13,192×, because the rule subtracts two nearby numbers and an 8-bit mantissa has nothing left after the cancellation. bf16 trains the model perfectly well — it just cannot reverse it.

5. Reversibility bought one doubling of batch, and the doubling was not worth having

Batch 32 fits only under reversibility. But throughput falls monotonically with batch on this device (10,246 → 9,488 tokens/s across 8 to 32) and the step count falls with it. The memory saving is real; there was nothing useful to spend it on.

6. The memory saving shrinks as batch grows

51.8% at batch 8, 20.0% at batch 16. Reversibility removes layer activations and does not touch the output head, which is batch × 1024 × 8192 × 4 bytes with three copies live. As batch grows the head dominates the peak.

7. Wall clock on a unified-memory machine measures the machine, not the method

Closing a browser produced a 14× speedup mid-run, from 10.36 to 0.72 s/step. Every throughput figure here comes from a quiet-machine measurement with mean/median near 1.00.

What this experiment cannot tell you

Limits, stated plainly
LimitDetail
one seedevery run used seed 1337. The 0.022% gap and the 1.10% euler advantage are single observations. Euler's margin is fifty times the midpoint gap so it is unlikely to be noise, but it is not established.
one model, one dataset20M parameters on TinyStories. The finding that a larger batch buys nothing is specific to being bandwidth-bound at this scale.
unscaled learning raterun 04 measures unscaled LR at 4× batch, not the best achievable result at batch 32.
allocator high-waterthe batch-8-to-16 jump is +5.09 GB, the 16-to-24 jump only +0.41 GB. Activations scale linearly with batch; those figures do not. The batch-8 comparison is sound — both variants under identical conditions — but the scaling curve is not.
batch 96the 20.02 GB figure is the peak reached before allocation failed, not a working set.
three single samplesbatches 48, 64 and 80 completed one step each. Their medians are real durations; their dispersion statistics are meaningless and are suppressed.

The one-sentence version

A reversible stack stores two states instead of every layer's activations, rebuilds the rest during the backward pass, and reaches the same loss for about a quarter more compute per step — which is worth having exactly when memory is the thing you are short of, and not otherwise.

Where to go next: the interesting open question is whether the per-step advantage at batch 32 survives a scaled learning rate. If it does, the larger batch stops being a consolation prize and becomes the point.
